Work Process

How I work

The same four stages on every project, in this order.

  1. 01

    Understand the Business

    What does this place make money from? For a restaurant it is completed paid orders; for a club it is booked tables. That becomes the thing the system is built around.

  2. 02

    Model the Data

    The schema comes before the first screen. Entities and the states they can be in — unpaid, paid, dispatched — because everything later is built on these tables.

  3. 03

    Build and Validate the Flows

    The paths a customer, promoter or admin actually walks. Integrations get wired here, and the transitions between them are tested against the failure cases, not just the happy path.

  4. 04

    Deploy, Monitor and Improve

    Ship to Vercel, then stay with it. Performance, image delivery and technical SEO get tuned after launch, and new requirements keep arriving.

The business problem

Al Qaysar has traded since 2015 and was already selling online — on foodpanda and Grab. The problem was what those platforms cost. Aggregator commission runs roughly 35–40% on every order, which either comes out of the margin or gets priced into the menu, so the customer pays more for the same food and the restaurant owns none of the relationship.

Anything ordered outside those apps was coordinated by hand between the customer, the kitchen and whoever could take the food out.

So the brief was not a website with a menu on it. It was a direct channel the restaurant owns and can price below the aggregators — with the same convenience customers now expect from foodpanda and Grab. That means the whole machinery: take the order, take payment for it, and get a courier to the door without anyone reconciling the three by hand.

The order workflow

  1. Customer builds an order

    Browses the menu by category with sorting, best-sellers, recommended items and favourites, adds to cart with per-item instructions, and chooses delivery or pickup. Guests can check out for pickup; delivery requires an account, because the delivery fee is tied to a saved, verified address.

  2. Delivery is quoted before payment

    For delivery orders the saved address is sent to Lalamove for a live quotation, so the customer sees the real courier fee at checkout rather than an estimate. The quotation is short-lived, so checkout re-verifies it server-side against the address on file and takes the fee from Lalamove's response — a client that tampers with the fee is rejected.

  3. Order and payment are created together, then handed to Xendit

    One database transaction writes the order, its line items with price snapshots, the delivery address snapshot and a PENDING payment record. The customer is redirected to a Xendit invoice for their chosen rail — GCash, bank transfer or card. If the gateway will not open a session the order is rolled back rather than left orphaned. A QUOTED delivery booking is recorded at this point: the quote is held, but no rider has been requested.

  4. Xendit webhook confirms the money

    The webhook is authenticated by callback token, tolerates several payload shapes, and checks the paid amount against the order total before accepting it. On success the payment becomes PAID and the order becomes CONFIRMED. Expired and failed invoices are recorded without ever downgrading a payment already marked paid.

  5. The kitchen starts preparing — and that books the rider

    A confirmed order reaches the admin dashboard already settled, so approval is a question of kitchen capacity rather than chasing payment. When staff start preparation, the same action places the Lalamove order against the held quotation. Cleared payment is the guard on that action, not the trigger for it — the restaurant decides when a rider is called, and a failed booking surfaces in the dashboard with a retry.

  6. Lalamove webhooks own the delivery leg

    Rider status — assigning driver, on-going, picked up, completed, rejected, expired — is written to the delivery booking as the single source of truth. The customer-facing kitchen status is derived from it in application code and never copied onto the order row, so the two cannot drift. The customer tracks Payment confirmed → Finding rider → Rider assigned → Picked up → Out for delivery → Delivered.

  7. Pickup skips the courier entirely

    Pickup orders run the same payment path but no booking is ever created, and the customer sees a different timeline: Order Confirmed → Restaurant Preparing → Ready for Pickup → Completed.

Table reservations are handled off-platform through a booking form, deliberately — dine-in never touches the order, payment or delivery models.

My responsibilities

  • Database architecture in Prisma for menu, carts, orders, payments, deliveries and addresses
  • Xendit integration: invoice creation, webhook authentication, amount verification, expiry and failure handling
  • Lalamove integration: quotation, server-side quote verification, order placement, webhook ingestion, retry and cancel
  • Role-based admin dashboard — orders, payments, delivery, products, categories, banners, customers, and reports with PDF/XLSX export
  • Better Auth with roles and permissions, account lockout, and guest or account checkout
  • Storefront: menu, cart, checkout, order tracking, and an address book with map pinning
  • Store availability controls, so the restaurant can stop taking orders on schedule or on demand
  • Deployment on Vercel and ongoing maintenance

Money moves through this system and a third-party courier is dispatched by it. The hard part was not either integration — it was deciding what each one is allowed to decide. Payment state, kitchen state and courier state are three separate records with one owner each, and the handoffs between them are explicit.

The business problem

Nearly all of the venue's revenue arrives as a table reservation, and promoters bring a large share of the guests. Both were tracked by hand — bookings scattered across message threads, and promoter commission reconciled manually at the end of each week.

Manual reconciliation is the real problem: commission has to be correct because somebody gets paid from it, and a hand-tallied number is impossible to audit. The site had to become the system the venue is operated from.

The reservation and commission flow

  1. Guest reserves a table

    Browses VIP packages, the bar-area map and the events calendar, then books — optionally through a promoter's invitation code, which is validated server-side.

  2. Table is allocated

    Staff see the reservation land in the dashboard and assign the actual table, zone and package tier against live availability.

  3. Guest is attributed to the promoter

    The invited guest stays linked to the promoter who brought them, and that link survives later changes to the booking.

  4. Cashier settles the bill

    POS subtotals are entered at settlement. The system separates regular from special orders and strips the 12% VAT to derive the true net figure.

  5. Commission is calculated on the VAT-exclusive base

    commission = (regular orders ÷ 1.12) × rate, where the rate is 7%, rising to 10% once the promoter's cumulative base passes ₱200,000. A super-admin can override the rate per promoter.

  6. Payout reporting

    Earnings move through PENDING → APPROVED → PAID, so every peso traces back to a specific settled transaction.

The guest list and door flow

  1. Guest registers

    Through a promoter's invitation link. Duplicate emails and mobile numbers are rejected globally.

  2. A signed QR token is issued

    With an expiry policy tied to the event or the club's operating hours.

  3. Door staff scan the QR

    Using the in-browser camera scanner, and the guest is checked in against the correct club night.

A club night runs 8PM–8AM, so “today” crosses midnight. Every date rule in the system resolves against the operating night, not the calendar day.

Roles and access

  • Public guest — no account. Books a table or registers for the guest list.
  • Promoter — own login and session. Invitation codes, referral attribution, earnings visibility, payout details.
  • Staff — four roles behind a 25-permission matrix:
    • Receptionist — reservations and guest list
    • Cashier — payment settlement and processing
    • Manager — operations, promoters, content
    • Super Admin — staff accounts, settings, commission overrides

Staff and promoters run on separate cookies and separate session systems, so a signed-in promoter can never reach staff surfaces, and vice versa.

My responsibilities

  • Data architecture — 29 models covering reservations, tables, payments, promoters, guests and customers
  • Two independent authentication systems with edge session validation and a per-route permission matrix
  • Commission engine: VAT-exclusive base, tiered rates, manual overrides, payout lifecycle
  • QR guest list end to end — token issuance, expiry policy, camera check-in
  • Admin dashboard: floor plan, table board, cashier, customer CRM, promoter administration
  • Content management so staff edit packages, drinks, events and gallery without a deploy
  • Multilingual public site and performance work; deployed on Vercel with Neon Postgres

The public pages are the smallest part of this build. Underneath sit two authentication systems, a 25-permission role matrix, and a commission chain that has to reconcile against VAT-exclusive transaction records — because somebody gets paid from it.
